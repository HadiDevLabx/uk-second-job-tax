Second job tax

Your second job is taxed from the first pound with no allowance, and the payslip looks punitive. Usually that is correct. Sometimes it is not — and the difference is worth knowing.

Why it happens

You get one personal allowance, not one per job. Your main employer applies the whole of it through a normal code like 1257L. The second employer has none left to give, so HMRC issues BR — basic rate on everything, from the first pound.

That is not a penalty for having two jobs. It is the system avoiding giving you the allowance twice, which would leave you underpaid and facing a bill. The tax code checker will tell you what any code on either job is doing.

The total is what matters, not the split. Two jobs paying £20,000 and £8,000 should cost the same tax as one job paying £28,000. If it does not, something in the codes is wrong — not the fact of the second job.

When BR is right, and when it is not

Your situationBR on job 2?What should happen
Main job pays over £12,570CorrectAllowance is fully used by job 1. Nothing to fix
Main job pays under £12,570OverpayingPart of your allowance is going unused. Ask HMRC to split it
Combined income over £50,270UnderpayingBR takes 20% where 40% is due. Expect a bill unless the code is changed to D0
Job 2 is your only job nowWrongJob 1 ended and HMRC has not caught up. Send the P45

The expensive case is the one that looks fine. If two jobs together push you over £50,270, BR deducts 20% on the second job where 40% is owed. Nothing looks wrong on either payslip, and the shortfall surfaces later as an underpayment collected through next year's code. Splitting your allowance across two jobs

If your main job pays less than the personal allowance, some of it is going to waste. HMRC can divide the allowance between employers — for example 600L on one and 657L on the other. You ask by contacting HMRC with both employers' PAYE references and an estimate of what each will pay.

Worth doing when the main job is part-time or seasonal. Not worth doing when the main job already exceeds the allowance, because there is nothing left to move. The hourly wage calculator is a quick way to estimate what a variable-hours job will actually pay over a year.

National Insurance works differently, and it is not in your favour

Income tax is cumulative across the year and self-corrects. National Insurance is not: it is calculated per job, per pay period, with each employment getting its own threshold.

Two jobs at £9,000 each pay little or no employee NI, because neither crosses the threshold on its own — a genuine advantage. But the reverse also holds: NI is never reconciled across jobs at year end the way income tax is, so you cannot reclaim it by filing. The National Insurance calculator shows the per-job position.

This is the one place two jobs can beat one. £18,000 from a single employer pays employee NI on everything above the threshold. Split across two employers at £9,000 each, much of it falls below both thresholds. Income tax treats the two cases identically; NI does not.

What to do if it looks wrong

  1. Add both gross figures together and check the total against what one job of that size would cost — the take-home pay calculator does this in one go.
  2. Check both tax codes. 1257L on one and BR on the other is the normal pattern; two codes with allowances is not, and will cause an underpayment.
  3. If a job has ended, make sure the P45 reached the other employer — a stale job 1 is the most common cause of a wrong code.
  4. If you have overpaid, the tax refund calculator estimates what is owed back. Income tax overpayments are refundable; NI generally is not.
  5. Check a specific payslip line by line with the payslip checker.

Related situations

  • Self-employed alongside a job — different again: the side income goes through Self Assessment, not PAYE, and your allowance is already spent
  • Overtime — often confused with a second job, but taxed cumulatively within one employment
  • A bonus — the same "why was so much taken" feeling, a different cause
  • Adjusted net income — combined income counts towards the £100,000 and £60,000 thresholds, which catches two-job households
  • Student loan — deducted per job above the threshold, so a second job under it pays nothing
  • Comparing offers — worth running before taking on the second job at all
